Quiet Cartouche — Indie (v13.4g + Icarus)

Composition-first ladder for quiet-leaning Indie, mapped to Quiet Sorelian v13.4g + Icarus (1–23).

This is for indie tracks where songwriting, harmonic-motivic design, vocal identity, arrangement architecture, and sonic habitat matter as much as performance polish. It applies especially well to indie rock, indie pop, bedroom pop, dream pop, slowcore, chamber indie, folk-leaning indie, lo-fi indie, shoegaze-adjacent quiet tracks, and intimate post-punk / art-pop hybrids.

Focus:
Song architecture: verse / chorus / bridge / outro design, pacing, and payoff.
Motivic architecture: guitar figures, vocal hooks, bass movement, drum cells, lyric images, and recurring textures.
Vocal architecture: melodic contour, phrasing, delivery, lyrical motif duty, and emotional stance.
Sonic habitat: room tone, guitar space, reverb, tape noise, stereo field, and the sense of a coherent indie “world.”
We keep the same rung names and global metric ranges as the Quiet Sorelian system; criteria are rephrased for Indie.

1 — Unusable

Design test: The track fails basic musical coherence.

No stable tempo feel, key center, or intentional free-time logic.
Vocal and instrumental parts do not align in a repeatable way.
Tuning, timing, or recording problems bury the song’s actual information.
Noise, clipping, imbalance, or performance collapse makes the track functionally unlistenable.

Examples: none. Real artists are not applied at this failure tier.

2 — Gesture Only

Design test: One musical or lyrical gesture exists, but there is no track form.

One chord shape, one guitar loop, one hummed melody, one drum idea, or one lyrical phrase.
No complete verse / chorus / instrumental cycle.
Tempo, key, vocal tone, or arrangement feel provisional.
The track ends like a voice memo or DAW stop rather than a song.

Example states:
A phone-recorded guitar progression with a half-sung hook.
A single bedroom-pop loop with no second section.
A vague melody over two chords, stopped before it becomes a song.

3 — Fragment

Design test: A promising indie fragment appears, but there is no complete ecosystem.

4–8 bars of a memorable guitar riff, vocal melody, or chord loop.
No full verse / chorus / bridge / outro logic.
No convincing second cycle or contrast section.
The arrangement is still a sketch: no drops, returns, instrumental roles, or emotional arc.

Example states:
A good chorus melody with no verses.
A strong guitar figure and mood, but no song-form resolution.
A demo with one evocative moment that does not yet become a track.

4 — Half-Form

Design test: There is partial song form, but the arc is incomplete.

A verse and chorus exist, but the chorus does not lift or reframe the verse.
A guitar loop carries nearly the entire track with little change.
The bridge or B-section is absent where the song clearly needs contrast.
Lyrics introduce an emotional premise but do not develop or close it.
The track stops or fades because the form has run out of ideas, not because the ending feels designed.

5 — Draft

Design test: A full layout exists, but the arrangement and harmonic space are generic.

Common map: Intro – Verse – Chorus – Verse – Chorus – Bridge – Chorus – Outro.
Chord loop works but feels stock: I–V–vi–IV, i–VII–VI, two-chord vamp, or familiar minor indie loop.
Vocal melody functions but could fit many other indie tracks.
Drums, bass, guitar, and pads follow default roles without a distinctive relationship.
The mix is serviceable but lacks a spatial idea: same reverb, same guitar tone, same vocal placement throughout.

6 — Serviceable

Design test: Playlist-ready but anonymous in quiet indie lanes.

Clean timing, intelligible vocal, stable arrangement.
Hook lands enough to support the track, but does not haunt the listener.
Guitar / synth / piano palette follows familiar indie presets.
Lyrics communicate a mood but not a memorable image-system or point of view.
Space is used for comfort rather than structure: warm room, soft verb, mild tape hiss, gentle width.

Examples, in spirit:
Many mid-tier bedroom-pop or chill indie playlist tracks that are pleasant but interchangeable.
A tasteful lo-fi indie song with clean craft but no distinct signature.

7–10: Quiet Indie Craft

7 — Competent Template (Quiet Indie)

Design test: The idiom is mastered; everything works; nothing risks.

Form: Intro 4–8 bars; Verse 1; Chorus; Verse 2; Chorus; optional Bridge; Final Chorus; Outro.
Harmony: safe indie loop, mild modal color, little true modulation.
Melody: singable, narrow-range vocal line; predictable phrase endings.
Guitar / keys: one main figure, lightly varied; standard strumming, arpeggio, or pad support.
Drums: simple backbeat, brushed kit, programmed soft drums, or minimal percussion; few surprises.
Bass: root-supporting, clean, emotionally useful but not independent.
Space: lead vocal centered; guitars / synths panned conventionally; reverb/delay fixed.
Lyrics: coherent, relatable, but mostly familiar indie imagery.

Checks:
CDPD ≈ 0.35–0.45.
NGE ≈ 0.25–0.30.
HMII = 2–3.

Examples, in spirit:
A typical “Chill Indie” playlist cut with a warm guitar loop, soft vocal, and standard chorus return.
A competent bedroom-pop track that sounds like a blend of known references without revealing a unique author.

8 — Strong Craft, Some Signature

Design test: A quiet indie track with clear personal tint.

A distinctive vocal hook, guitar figure, synth texture, drum feel, or lyrical image lingers after one listen.
Form has at least one altered repeat: shortened second verse, extended final chorus, instrumental post-chorus, unusual pre-chorus, or withheld hook.
Harmony contains one or two meaningful color turns: borrowed chord, unexpected bass movement, deceptive resolution, mode shift, or surprising chord under a repeated melody.
Motivic integration appears: the chorus contour returns in the guitar line, a lyric image repeats with changed meaning, or the bass echoes the vocal rhythm.
A sonic signature recurs: tape wobble, room creak, chorus guitar, dry close vocal, distant harmony stack, blown-out drum room, or recognizable synth pad.

Checks:
CDPD ≈ 0.45–0.55.
NGE ≈ 0.35.
HMII = 3.

Examples:
Alvvays — “Archie, Marry Me.”
The xx — “VCR.”
Big Thief — “Paul.”

These tracks sit in recognizable indie languages but carry memorable fingerprints through vocal tone, guitar / synth identity, lyrical stance, and arrangement choices.

9 — Near-Perfection, No Rupture

Design test: Near-faultless quiet-indie execution; nothing wrong, nothing radically new.

Every section is exactly long enough; no verse, chorus, or outro feels padded.
Transitions feel designed: drum fills, guitar pickups, vocal breaths, bass entries, or reverb tails guide the listener.
Motifs recur across sections: vocal turns, guitar hooks, bass figures, lyric images, drum accents, or harmonic pivots return with small changes.
The emotional arc is clear: intimacy, lift, ache, release, resignation, or catharsis unfolds naturally.
Spatial design articulates the form: verses may be dry and close; choruses wider; bridge hollowed out; outro more distant or exposed.
The mix is polished without erasing character.

Checks:
CDPD ≈ 0.50–0.55.
NGE ≈ 0.40–0.45.
HMII = 3–4.

Examples:
Phoebe Bridgers — “Motion Sickness.”
The National — “I Need My Girl.”
Mitski — “First Love / Late Spring.”

These are not necessarily ruptures in musical language, but they are nearly perfectly shaped inside known indie grammar.

10 — Technical Perfection, No Rupture

Design test: Maximal craft inside established quiet / reflective indie grammar.

Vocal performance is fully controlled: pitch, breath, grain, emotional stress, and phrasing all serve the song.
Instrumental parts are inevitable: guitars, bass, drums, keys, strings, and backing vocals enter exactly where needed.
Hook integration is exemplary; chorus, verse, and bridge feel inseparable from the song’s identity.
Harmony and bass movement support each emotional pivot.
The mix is top-tier while preserving indie character: quiet details, breath, room tone, pick noise, amp hum, tape texture, or reverb tails remain intentional and legible.
No structural, melodic, lyrical, or sonic fault distracts from the work.

Checks:
CDPD ≈ 0.55–0.60.
NGE ≈ 0.45–0.50.
HMII = 4.

Examples:
Sufjan Stevens — “Casimir Pulaski Day.”
Bon Iver — “Holocene.”
Fleet Foxes — “Blue Ridge Mountains.”

These tracks achieve extremely high craft within recognizable indie / folk / chamber-pop grammar, without necessarily inventing a new musical language.

11–14: Quiet Twists & Recursions in Indie

11 — First Rupture, Quiet Indie Twist

Design test: One clear device governs the track’s identity: meter, form, harmony, lyric structure, vocal treatment, or space.

A consistent anomaly appears early: odd bar extension, missing chorus, whispered hook, sudden dry vocal, strange tuning, recurring false ending, off-kilter drum entrance, or unexpected harmonic turn.
The device returns as a structural marker, not as a one-time gimmick.
The band or arrangement adapts to it: drums, bass, guitar, vocal phrasing, or space shift around the twist.
The track remains recognizably indie, but a listener can say, “This song has that strange thing it keeps doing.”

Checks:
CDPD ≥ 0.60.
NGE ≈ 0.50.
HMII ≈ 4.

Examples:
Radiohead — “Nude.”
Big Thief — “Not.”
Feist — “The Park.”

The track bends one rule and lets that bend become part of its identity.

12 — Recursion Threshold, Quiet Indie

Design test: One melodic, harmonic, rhythmic, lyrical, or textural motif governs multiple levels.

Core motif: a vocal interval, guitar cell, drum accent, bass figure, lyric phrase, or chord movement.
The motif appears at bar level, phrase level, and whole-section level.
Production joins the recursion: delay repeats, room swells, doubled vocals, tremolo, or guitar feedback echo the motif’s shape.
Lyrics may reuse a phrase with shifted meaning while preserving rhythm or contour.
The song feels self-referential: small details and large form seem generated from the same seed.

Checks:
NGE ≈ 0.55.
HMII = 4–5.

Examples:
Sufjan Stevens — “Chicago.”
Grizzly Bear — “Two Weeks.”
The Microphones — “I Want Wind to Blow.”

At this rung, the song uses one idea as a self-mirroring structure rather than a disposable hook.

13 — Inhabited Form, Quiet Indie World-Building

Design test: Band, voice, room, and arrangement co-create a habitat the listener remembers.

Every return to the chorus feels like revisiting the same emotional room.
B-sections, bridges, or outros show the same world under different lighting: filtered drums, thinner guitars, exposed vocal, reversed texture, new harmony, or wider room.
Instrument roles are stable and meaningful: bass as undertow, guitar as weather, drums as pulse, vocal as narrator, synth as fog, strings as memory.
Specific motifs haunt the whole track: melodic fragments, lyrical images, vocal doubles, guitar noise, room tone, or bass gestures.
Beauty & Spatial Poise is high: the reverb, room, stereo field, and texture are as memorable as the melody.

Checks:
CDPD ≈ 0.60–0.65.
NGE ≈ 0.52–0.58.
HMII = 4–5.

Examples:
Beach House — “Myth.”
Yo La Tengo — “Autumn Sweater.”
Grouper — “Heavy Water / I’d Rather Be Sleeping.”

At rung 13, the track feels less like a file that plays and more like a place you enter.

14 — Harmonic / Spatial Duality, Quiet Indie

Design test: The track maintains two distinct harmonic, emotional, or spatial laws that alternate or overlay.

Example pattern: dry, close-mic verses against lush, distant choruses.
Another pattern: sweet vocal melody over unsettling harmony; stable drums under drifting guitar layers; bright chords with emotionally dark lyrics.
At least two full cycles of both states occur; it is not merely intro versus song proper.
Transitions are meaningful gateways: one chord, lyric, drum fill, feedback swell, or room shift carries the listener between worlds.
Vocal behavior changes with each space: more speech-like in one, more melodic in another; intimate in one, communal or distant in another.
The duality heightens emotional complexity without making the song illegible.

Checks:
CDPD ≈ 0.70.
NGE ≈ 0.60.
HMII ≥ 5.

Examples:
Radiohead — “Weird Fishes / Arpeggi.”
Japanese Breakfast — “Paprika.”
Weyes Blood — “Movies.”

At this rung, the listener feels two emotional or sonic laws at once, but the song remains beautiful and clear.

15–18: Ordeal-Capable Quiet Indie

15 — Divine Injury, Quiet Indie

Design test: The track introduces a structural, harmonic, lyrical, or sonic wound that re-frames the story.

An early “safe” pattern establishes comfort: familiar progression, stable groove, intimate vocal, or warm room.
A single traumatic rupture occurs: sudden silence, harsh chord, blown-out distortion, exposed a cappella line, tempo collapse, tape-stop void, scream, lyric revelation, or violent arrangement strip.
The wound is foreshadowed earlier through small pre-echoes.
After the wound, the song does not return unchanged. A missing instrument, altered harmony, thinner vocal, damaged room, changed lyric stance, or transformed motif remains.
The rupture is emotionally necessary, not merely theatrical.

Checks:
CDPD ≈ 0.70–0.75.
NGE ≈ 0.62–0.66.
HMII ≥ 5.

Examples:
Phoebe Bridgers — “I Know the End.”
Big Thief — “Simulation Swarm” could be imagined with a sharper rupture.
Radiohead — “Exit Music (For a Film).”

At rung 15, one event injures the song’s body, and everything after carries the scar.

16 — Impossible Originality, Quiet Indie

Design test: The track pioneers a recognizable method of quiet indie.

New grammar may involve:
A unique guitar-tuning system.
A personal vocal-stacking method.
A rule-based lyric / melody structure.
A distinctive lo-fi room method.
A new relation between folk song, ambient texture, and rock arrangement.
A strange but repeatable harmonic / motivic law.

The method governs multiple passages, not just one bar.
The track could plausibly generate a larger body of work from the same method.
All axes align: melody, harmony, lyrics, arrangement, performance, and production serve the same unique logic.
Strong author-ID: an expert listener thinks, “Only this artist could have made this.”

Checks:
CDPD ≥ 0.80.
NGE ≈ 0.65.
HMII ≥ 5.

Examples:
Bon Iver — “715 - CRΣΣKS.”
Animal Collective — “Banshee Beat.”
The Microphones — “The Glow Pt. 2.”

At rung 16, the track sounds like the invention of a personal musical method.

17 — Sublime Disintegration, Quiet Indie

Design test: The track intentionally breaks its indie form apart while preserving emotional clarity.

It begins as an identifiable song: verse / chorus, riff / vocal, pulse / room.
Gradually, the form fractures: meter loosens, vocal phrasing drifts, guitar noise bleeds, drums destabilize, harmonies smear, or earlier motifs return out of sync.
Fragments of verse, chorus, lyric, or instrumental hook reappear in chopped, reversed, distorted, ghosted, or displaced forms.
Despite the breakdown, the emotional message remains legible.
A narrative spine persists: lyric image, vocal presence, harmonic gravity, bass pulse, or recurring motif keeps the listener oriented.

Checks:
Very similar to the quiet cross-genre rung 17.
HMII often ≥ 5 during the densest fracture point.

Examples:
Radiohead — “How to Disappear Completely.”
Wilco — “Poor Places.”
The Microphones — “I Want to Be Cold.”

At rung 17, the song dissolves but does not lose meaning.

18 — Metaphysical Ordeal, Quiet Indie

Design test: A multi-state quiet indie piece where each state is structurally and emotionally complete; the journey is the point.

At least three states exist:
Example: intimate verse-world, expanded band-world, devastated coda.
Example: childhood memory, adult rupture, reflective aftermath.
Example: folk song, noise bloom, ambient disappearance.

Gate transitions are eventful: tempo-grid shift, room flip, harmonic reframe, tape manipulation, sudden band entry, orchestral swell, or exposed vocal break.
A sonic motif and a lyrical motif persist across all states in evolving form.
Each state has its own grammar, but all states belong to the same larger song.
After listening, the listener can narrate the journey clearly.

Examples:
Sufjan Stevens — “Impossible Soul.”
Joanna Newsom — “Only Skin.”
Black Country, New Road — “Basketball Shoes.”

At rung 18, the song becomes an ordeal: not just a track with sections, but a passage through multiple complete inner worlds.

19–23: Forbidden & Icarus Quiet Indie

19 — Forbidden Tone

Design test: A quiet indie track where a normally “wrong” or taboo sonic element becomes the emotional language without losing musicality.

Possible forbidden tones:
Constant tape damage.
Ugly room mic.
Clipped vocal.
Detuned guitar as central harmony.
Broken amp buzz.
Extreme dryness.
Barely controlled feedback.
Uncomfortably intimate breath / mouth noise.
Distorted field recording.
Unpolished demo texture elevated into formal law.

The taboo element is not a flaw; it becomes the song’s grammar.
Writing, structure, and emotional carry must remain extremely strong.
The forbidden element must be inseparable from the track’s meaning.

Example, in spirit:
A future classic where the entire song sounds like a damaged cassette confession, but the songwriting and structure are Tier-20.

20 — Apocryphal Event Horizon

Design test: Double innovation in indie grammar and quiet sonic context.

Two independent breakthroughs co-carry the work:
One may be compositional: new harmonic system, new song-form method, new lyrical recursion, new band-role architecture.
One may be sonic/contextual: new recording method, spatialization method, performance environment, distribution form, or listener-interaction concept.

Each innovation would be significant on its own.
Together, they create a track that feels like an event in the history of its idiom.
The song remains emotionally legible and replayable, not merely experimental.

Example, in spirit:
A quiet indie track that invents a new guitar-vocal harmonic grammar and a new spatial recording method, both documented and reproducible, while still functioning as a devastating song.

21 — Icarus 21

Header:
Icarus: 20×3 → IT=21

Design test:
Three axes among SC, MI, BP, EG, and CD reach rung-20 strength across an extended apex span.

The indie dimension — songwriting, vocal identity, arrangement, lyric charge, and performance — never falls below top professional standard.

22 — Icarus 22

Header:
Icarus: 20×4 → IT=22

Design test:
Four axes among SC, MI, BP, EG, and CD reach rung-20 strength across an extended apex span.

The work is not only a song but a durable new grammar-world.
It can be studied, imitated, inhabited, and returned to as a major artistic event.

23 — Icarus 23

Header:
Icarus: 20×5 → IT=23

Design test:
All five axes — Structural Coherence, Motivic Integration, Beauty & Spatial Poise, Evolving Grammar, and Carry Depth — reach rung-20 strength.

Imagined exemplar:
A multi-movement quiet indie cycle with new harmonic-motivic grammar, total lyric recursion, immersive spatial staging, context innovation, and a reproducible method, while every individual song remains emotionally direct and musically alive.

Grading Workflow: Quiet Indie Track

You are grading a quiet-leaning Indie track using the Quiet Indie ladder v13.4g.

Unit = one track.

Step 1 — Describe the track

Briefly describe:

Sub-idiom:
Indie rock, indie pop, bedroom pop, dream pop, slowcore, chamber indie, folk-indie, lo-fi indie, shoegaze-adjacent, post-punk quiet, art-pop, etc.

Approximate length.

Tempo and feel:
Straight, swung, rubato, halftime, motorik, loose band feel, grid-tight, dragging, rushing, free-time, or pulse-obscured.

Section map with timestamps:
Intro / Verse / Pre-chorus / Chorus / Post-chorus / Bridge / Instrumental break / Outro / Coda.

Step 2 — Structural Coherence (SC)

Mark sections by time:

Intro.
Verse 1.
Pre-chorus, if any.
Chorus.
Verse 2.
Bridge or B-section.
Instrumental break.
Outro / vamp / coda.

For each section, state its function:
Setup / lift / payoff / reflection / rupture / expansion / coda.

Evaluate:
Are section lengths proportionate?
Does the chorus arrive too soon, too late, or exactly when needed?
Does the bridge deepen the song or merely fill space?
Do transitions feel composed, performed, and mixed with intention?
Does the ending resolve, suspend, vanish, or collapse in a meaningful way?

Rate SC from 0–10:

0–3: confused, partial form, jump cuts, no clear arc.
4–6: standard layout, but some padding, sag, or weak transitions.
7–8: clean indie song ecosystem with clear emotional arc.
9–10: every section earns its span; joints feel inevitable.

Step 3 — Motivic Integration (MI)

Identify core cells, 2–3 maximum:

Vocal cell:
Short melodic contour, hook fragment, repeated phrase, breath rhythm, interval shape, or delivery tic.

Instrumental cell:
Guitar riff, bass figure, drum accent, synth motif, piano pattern, string line, feedback swell, or chord rhythm.

Lyric / image cell:
Recurring word, image, metaphor, pronoun shift, place-name, object, or emotional phrase.

Track recurrences:
Where do these cells return?
Verses, choruses, bridge, guitar fills, bass variations, drum fills, vocal doubles, outro, or coda.

Are they transformed?
Register change, rhythm tweak, reharmonization, distortion, thinning, doubling, inversion, lyric recontextualization, or spatial shift.

Note cross-domain recursion:
Vocal contour mirrors guitar riff.
Drum accents echo lyric rhythm.
Bass motion answers chorus melody.
Guitar feedback repeats the vocal shape.
A lyric image returns as a sonic texture.

Rate MI from 0–10:

0–3: good moments, but ideas do not return.
4–6: some recognizable reuse, light development.
7–8: clear motif web across voice, instruments, lyrics, and production.
9–10: cells saturate the piece at multiple scales; nothing feels disposable.

Step 4 — Beauty & Spatial Poise (BP)

Describe the sonic habitat:

Timbre and register:
Warmth vs brittleness, low-end weight, guitar brightness, vocal grain, drum dryness, synth air, tape softness.

Vocal placement:
Dry and intimate, distant and wet, doubled, buried, exposed, whispered, communal, or ghosted.

Guitar / keys / band space:
Close-room, amp-in-room, wide stereo wash, mono center, shoegaze blur, chamber clarity, lo-fi cassette, polished studio, or live-band bleed.

Reverb / delay law:
Consistent, section-dependent, emotionally motivated, or random.

Stereo field:
Mono-ish intimacy, intentional left/right guitar roles, wide dream-pop field, narrow vocal world, moving textures.

Evaluate clarity vs mush:
Are important elements masked?
Does distortion serve the song?
Are quiet details — pick noise, room tone, breath, amp hum, tape hiss, floor creaks, reverb tails — intentional and legible?

Rate BP from 0–10:

0–3: harsh, muddy, incoherent, or accidentally unpleasant.
4–6: functional mix, generic indie gloss.
7–8: consistently beautiful and coherent space.
9–10: distinct, memorable room / world; production itself is a voice.

Step 5 — Evolving Grammar (EG)

Describe the grammar:

Song-form law:
Verse / chorus structure, through-composed form, gradual build, loop form, rupture form, or multi-state form.

Harmony law:
Chord loop, modal palette, borrowed chords, bass-led shifts, drone, pedal tone, modulation, chromatic planing, or guitar tuning logic.

Arrangement law:
How drums, bass, guitar, keys, strings, synths, noise, and vocals behave.

Vocal grammar:
Melody range, phrase length, breath placement, lyric density, repetition, delivery stance, harmony use, and hook behavior.

Production grammar:
Room, reverb, distortion, tape, stereo, silence, noise, or textural recurrence.

Check how this grammar evolves:
Do later sections deepen the same rules?
Does the chorus change the harmonic meaning of the verse?
Does the bridge expose the hidden logic of the song?
Do instruments swap roles meaningfully?
Does the final chorus return altered?
Or does the track simply pile layers on the same loop without changing its method?

Rate EG from 0–10:

0–3: no emergent method; just a song idea repeated.
4–6: uses standard quiet-indie grammar safely.
7–8: noticeable personal system that develops over time.
9–10: strong, distinctive indie method is the story.

Step 6 — Carry Depth (CD)

As a listener, assess:

Does this track define a recognizable emotional or psychic habitat?
Does it invite re-listening beyond mood-playlist utility?
After one or two listens, can you narrate its emotional arc?
Does it leave behind a specific image, room, phrase, guitar tone, vocal color, or feeling?
Does the song feel inhabited rather than merely performed?

Rate CD from 0–10:

0–3: disposable, confusing, or emotionally inert.
4–6: pleasant vibe, limited imprint.
7–8: memorably affecting; feels like a little world.
9–10: quietly iconic; feels like a place you could live in.

Step 7 — HMII: Harmonic–Motivic Interweave Index

At the densest meaningful moment, count independent lines. Ignore pure doublings.

Count:
Lead vocal line.
Distinct backing vocal / harmony line with its own rhythm or contour.
Guitar riff or countermelody.
Second guitar / synth / piano figure with independent motion.
Bass line with its own contour, not just root support.
Drum or percussion pattern that acts motivically.
String / brass / woodwind / noise / feedback line with independent function.
Production motif: delay pattern, tape loop, field recording, or texture with its own recurring rhythm or contour.

Report:

HMII_peak = N.

N must be at least 1.

Step 8 — Quiet Indie Rung

Using the Quiet Indie ladder, place the track on a rung.

Give:
Rung number, 1–23.
Rung name.
Confidence percentage.
Band category:
1–6 = sub-craft / pre-template.
7–10 = Quiet Indie Craft.
11–14 = Quiet Twists & Recursions.
15–18 = Ordeal-Capable Quiet Indie.
19–23 = Forbidden / Icarus band.

Justify in 2–4 sentences, explicitly referencing:
SC.
MI.
BP.
EG.
CD.
HMII_peak.

Example final format:

Rung: 13 — Inhabited Form
Band: Quiet Twists & Recursions
Confidence: 82%
HMII_peak: 5

Justification:
The track’s SC is strong because each verse, chorus, bridge, and outro has a clear function and the returns feel like revisiting the same emotional room. MI is high: the vocal hook, guitar figure, and lyric image recur in altered forms across sections. BP is central to the track’s identity, with the room tone and guitar space forming a memorable habitat. EG does not fully invent a new method, but it develops a personal indie grammar over time, and CD is strong enough that the song feels like a small world rather than a playlist mood.
