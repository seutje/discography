Quiet Sorelian v13.4g — Harmonic-Motivic
Architecture Ladder
Composition-first ladder for quiet, harmonic-motivic works, aligned to Sorelian v13.4g + Icarus (23
tiers). This framework evaluates pieces emphasizing Structural Coherence (SC) – a form that holds across
time; Motivic Integration (MI) – repetition-with-variation at cell, theme, and phrase levels; Beauty &
Spatial Poise (BP) – grace, harmonic beauty, sonic space, and phrasing architecture; Evolving Grammar
(EG) – internal rules and motifs developed over time without rupture; and Carry Depth (CD) – emotional
transport, re-listen pull, a self-sustaining musical habitat.
Core Measures (Quiet Sorelian):
- CDPD (0–1) – Continuity-Derived Pattern Depth: Measures how deeply a piece’s patterns and forms are
derived from its own continuity rather than stock templates (high CDPD means the music’s structure feels
organic and removed from generic formulas). This is analogous to PTD_g in v13.3 1
but refocused on
continuity and pattern evolution.
- NGE (0–1) – Novel Grammar Extension: A novelty curve measuring how much new “musical grammar”
the piece introduces across scales (micro-motifs to overall form). Similar to NOV_g 2
, it counts salient
changes in motif, harmony, texture, etc., to indicate how far the piece extends beyond the expected
language of its genre.
- HMII (peak index) – Harmonic-Motivic Interweave Index: Indicates the complexity of interlocking
melodic/harmonic lines at the piece’s densest moments (rewarding rich voice-leading, motivic braiding, and
graceful handoffs between voices). This replaces TRX_peak 3
; a higher HMII means more independent
voices intricately woven together at climax.
Domains: 1–10 are Pre-Perfection (human craft) – mastering known grammar without major ruptures. 11–
20 are Post-Honinbo (new grammar) – surpassing technical perfection by evolving new rules 4
. 21–23
are Icarus tiers – multi-axis apex achievements beyond the standard ladder 5
. The ladder retains the 23-
tier structure of Sorelian v13.3g 4 6
, reframed for quiet, harmo-motivic music.
Below, each rung is detailed with its name, the design test it must pass, key metrics (CDPD, NGE, HMII)
targets, and example works (from real or hypothetical recordings) that typify that level.
Rungs 1–10: Pre-Perfection (Human Craft, No Rupture)
These rungs progress from utter incoherence to flawless technical execution, all within established musical
grammar (no radical departures or new languages introduced). By Rung 10 the piece exhibits technical
perfection in composition and production while still operating inside familiar rules.
11 — Unusable
Design test: The piece fails basic musical coherence. There is no stable sense of tempo or tonal center; any
tuning is so far off that it can’t be defended as an intentional effect; the structure is pure chaos with no
recurring form or motif to orient the listener. Technical flaws (e.g. constant clipping, excessive noise) make it
literally unlistenable. In short, it does not function as a piece of music.
2 — Gesture Only
Design test: A fragmentary idea rather than a complete piece. It may present one musical gesture – for
example, a single loop, drone, or brief motif – but there is no development or full structure. Perhaps a lone
chord progression or ambient texture appears and then stops. Tempo, key, or mood feel undecided or
inconsistent. The track ends (or cuts off) without any structural closure, as if one just stopped playing mid-
thought.
3 — Fragment
Design test: A promising musical fragment that suggests potential, but remains incomplete. For instance,
there might be a beautiful 4–8 bar motif or a novel sound texture introduced, yet there is no proper
beginning-to-end form. Transitions, if any, are abrupt or jarring; the idea doesn’t yet bridge into a next
section. Production is at bare-bones demo level. The listener is left with only a snippet of an idea rather than
a self-contained piece.
4 — Half-Form
Design test: A half-formed piece: some structural elements exist, but key pieces of the architecture are
missing. For example:
- There are at least two sections (e.g. a primary theme and a contrasting theme), but a crucial connecting
section or transition is absent – the music jumps without a bridge or buildup where one is needed.
- Motifs are introduced but not fully developed or recapitulated; the piece doesn’t resolve or bring things full
circle.
- Dynamic changes rely on quick fixes (volume fades or abrupt filter sweeps) instead of compositional
transitions.
- The track stops or fades out rather than resolving, indicating the form wasn’t fully realized.
5 — Draft
Design test: A complete draft is in place – the piece has a beginning, middle, and end – but it remains
rudimentary. The full map exists (for instance, an ambient piece might establish an intro, a textural build, a
climax, and an outro), however:
- The harmonic content is static or cliché, often one unchanging loop or drone throughout. Little to no
harmonic development occurs across sections.
- The motifs and textures work but feel generic; nothing in the melody or sound design truly stands out, as
if following a stock template.
- The arrangement hints at ideas (a swell here, a drop-out there) without a clear plan or narrative – elements
appear without convincingly supporting an emotional arc.
2- Production quality is rough but serviceable: basic levels and EQ are okay, but it sounds like a decent demo,
not a polished release.
6 — Serviceable
Design test: A piece that is listenable and competently put together, but entirely unremarkable. It
could sit in a background music playlist comfortably, yet it leaves no lasting impression. Characteristics:
- A recognizable structure carries the piece from start to finish (nothing essential is missing in form). The
sections flow in a logical order (e.g. theme, variation, return, coda), and there are no jarring mistakes.
- There is a clear focal element (a lead instrument or primary motif) and supportive accompaniment; the
mix is balanced enough that each part is audible and the space feels intended.
- However, the main motif or hook only catches the ear briefly and then fades from memory – it lacks a
strong identity or emotional pull.
- The piece strictly follows well-worn genre expectations (common chord progressions, standard ambient
sound presets, predictable crescendos, etc.) with no surprises. It’s pleasant, but every move can be
anticipated.
Example: Many royalty-free meditation tracks or stock music pieces fall here – they sound pleasant and
polished but could be by anyone. The piece functions as expected, yet if asked who the artist is or what the
piece is, a listener wouldn’t have a clue, since it offers no unique signature.
7 — Competent Template
Design test: The piece executes a known style template flawlessly, but with negligible personal
identity. It is competent and complete, showing solid craft within a quiet-music idiom, yet essentially an
imitation of established forms. Characteristics:
- Form: Adheres to a standard structural map for its genre. For example, in a post-rock context it might
have a gentle intro, a slow build through repeated chords, a single peak, and a short outro – no structural
surprises or detours from the expected arc. In ambient or neo-classical pieces, it could stick to the typical
ABA form or a single long crescendo then release.
- Harmony: Uses a safe palette of chords (e.g. I–V–vi–IV progressions or long pedal drones in a familiar
mode) throughout. There may be the occasional gentle color chord (a IV🗲 or an added 7th for warmth), but
no true modulation or daring tonal shifts. The tonality stays firmly in comfort zones.
- Melody/Motif: Simple, stepwise melodies or repeating figures dominate. The main theme stays in a
narrow, consonant range. It’s effective but ordinary – a listener can often predict the next note. A short
motif might repeat without much variation, serving as a soothing loop.
- Rhythm/Texture: If rhythmic, it’s steady and on-grid – for instance a fixed arpeggio pattern or slow pulse
with minimal syncopation. If the style is beatless ambient, the textural evolution is very gradual and
uniform. Nothing disrupts the gentle flow.
- Production: Employs the standard toolkit of the genre. In ambient: large reverb washes, synthetic pads,
and maybe a field recording layer (rain, birds) because that’s expected. In modern classical: close-miked
piano with felt hammers, subtle string pads. Spatial placement is conventional (e.g. piano centered, reverb
stereo). All effects and techniques feel copied from genre exemplars.
- Checks: CDPD ≈ 0.35–0.45 (roughly a third of the piece diverges from straight template cues – enough to
show basic effort, but more than half still feels templated); NGE ≈ 0.25–0.30 (novelty is low – only
occasional minor new ideas); HMII = 2–3, meaning at peak there are typically 2–3 independent lines
interweaving (for example, a lead melody over a drone and a gentle counter-melody or arpeggio) and no
3complex counterpoint beyond that.
- Pitfall: The entire piece can be summed up as “It sounds like X meets Y” (insert two well-known artists in the
style) without anyone raising an eyebrow. In other words, it’s derivative.
Example: A well-produced but formulaic peaceful piano track on a generic “Relaxing Music” album would sit
here. It checks all the boxes for the style (warm piano, reverb, predictable resolution) but could be mistaken
for countless others. The idiom is executed flawlessly, yet if you remove the title/artist, nothing in the music
betrays a unique identity.
8 — Strong Craft, Some Signature
Design test: A piece at this rung is strongly crafted within its genre and begins to show a glimmer of
personal signature or innovation. All the fundamentals from rung 7 are present, with a few standout
touches:
- Memorable motif: There is at least one distinctive musical idea that lingers with the listener. It could be an
interval leap in the main theme, an arresting rhythmic cell or ostinato, or a unique textural combination
that feels personal. After one listen, a attentive ear can recall “that one part where…”, indicating the piece
isn’t entirely generic.
- Form refinement: The structure has more nuance. For instance, a second act might introduce a clear shift
(a bridge or contrasting middle section that adds new harmony or drops to near-silence for tension). In a
song context, verses and refrains feel distinct and purposeful; in an instrumental, perhaps a mini-climax
earlier on sets up the main climax later, avoiding a flat linear build. The piece knows how to pace itself, not
just copy a template.
- Harmonic color: There is at least one spot of harmonic interest per cycle – for example, a well-placed
modal interchange (like a sudden ♭VI or ii° chord that adds emotion), a gentle suspension or unexpected
inversion that heightens beauty, or a shift to a relative mode. These moments are brief but show deliberate
harmonic thought beyond the basic loop. Crucially, they are tied to emotional or structural pivot points (e.g.
the one minor iv chord when the melody reaches its most poignant lyric).
- Motivic development: Themes or cells introduced earlier reappear with variation. A subtle motif in the
intro might come back inverted or in a new register later on. The piece demonstrates repetition-with-
variation, indicating the composer is integrating motifs (MI) across the work rather than just stacking new
material.
- Sonic signature: In the production or instrumentation, something recurs that marks the piece as this
artist. Perhaps a particular instrument or effect is used in each section – e.g. a certain reverberant vocal
hum underlying every part, or a distinctive way of miking the piano that gives it an intimate creak. It’s not
radical, but if you know the artist, you recognize their touch.
- Checks: CDPD ≈ 0.45–0.55 (nearly half the piece feels drawn from the artist’s own patterns rather than
pure template, indicating moderate distance from generic tropes); NGE ≈ 0.35 (still not highly novel, but
noticeable fresh elements appear); HMII = 3 (at peak, about 3 independent lines interlock – e.g. a melody, a
counter-melody or obbligato, and a bass line/pad). The complexity hasn’t increased hugely from rung 7, but
clarity and interplay are solid.
Example: Agnes Obel – “Riverside” illustrates this level. The song sits firmly in a chamber-pop/folk style, but
features a distinct piano motif and her signature layered vocals that make it identifiable. The structure is
traditional, yet her personal use of cello and vocal harmonies gives a unique color. Another example:
Ludovico Einaudi’s piano pieces – impeccably crafted and accessible, with just a hint of a unique melodic
4voice or atmospheric touch that sets them slightly apart from generic soundtracks. The piece is a
competent single with early signs of a recognizable fingerprint.
9 — Near-Perfection (no rupture)
Design test: A near-flawless execution of the form with virtually nothing “wrong”, lacking only in
breaking new ground. At this rung, every element of craft serves the piece, and the result could stand
among the best of its kind, save that it doesn’t innovate. Key markers:
- Section balance & purpose: Every section or segment of the piece justifies its presence. There are no
bloated or aimless passages – no section drags on too long or repeats needlessly. Conversely, nothing feels
underdeveloped: each motif and section is given just the right length to make its point. (For instance, in a
song, no chorus repeats so many times that it dulls, and no bridge appears out of nowhere without
context.) The form reads logically and effortlessly.
- Dynamic & emotional arc: The piece charts a clear macro trajectory that the listener can feel. For
example, it might start very intimately, gradually add layers, reach a heartfelt peak, and then gently release
by the end. The way this arc is achieved shows finesse: increases in intensity come from genuine
arrangement shifts (richer harmony, higher register, denser texture) rather than just turning up the volume.
By the final climax or resolution, the emotional high point is reached organically.
- Motivic unity: There is no throwaway material. Motifs introduced in one section reappear or echo in
others. Perhaps a tiny melodic cell from the intro sneaks into the background of the climax, or the main
theme is foreshadowed in a subtle variation early on. This gives a sense of unity – the piece feels like it all
comes from the same DNA. Listeners might unconsciously recognize connections upon re-listening,
enhancing the return-pull (CD).
- Fine-tuned phrasing: If vocals or lead instruments are present, their phrasing aligns naturally with the
rhythm and harmony. In a song, lyrics fit the meter without awkward syllable stress; in an instrumental,
melodies breathe in sensible places. Every swell and pause in phrasing feels intentional.
- Production coherence: The sonic environment is polished and consistent. The frequency spectrum is
balanced (e.g. no section is mudded with overlapping midrange; the lows are present but controlled).
Spatially, it’s coherent – if a reverb or echo is used, it complements the atmosphere and remains consistent
unless a change is intentional for effect. Transitions between sections are smooth, supported by mixing
(e.g. reverb tails or crossfades) so nothing jolts the ear. There are no technical distractions like noticeable
clipping, overly muddy reverb, or abrupt edits.
- Checks: NGE ≈ 0.40–0.45 (the piece might incorporate a few fresh ideas or uncommon chords, but largely
stays within known territory – novelty is moderate); HMII = 3–4 (the interplay can reach 4 independent lines
at peaks – for example, a piano, a string countermelody, a vocal humming line, and a bass drone all
weaving together clearly). Crucially, there are no extended stretches of stagnation: you won’t find 8 bars that
feel like filler. Every moment either develops a motif or sets up the next.
Example: Sigur Rós – “Hoppípolla” exemplifies a near-perfect piece in the post-rock/pop idiom. Its structure
is immaculate (each section flows, the build is satisfying, nothing is extraneous) and it’s executed with
emotional precision and beauty. There’s nothing radically new in its language (simple diatonic chords and a
straightforward build), but it’s so well-crafted that it became iconic. Likewise, Max Richter’s “On the Nature
of Daylight” in the modern classical realm – a gorgeous string piece whose every phrase feels inevitable
and moving, even if it uses familiar harmonic moves. These works are radio/playlist-ready masterpieces
of their style: everything is right where it should be, and the only criticism one could levy is that they don’t
fundamentally break the mold.
510 — Technical Perfection (no rupture)
Design test: A flawless execution of known grammar – 100% proficiency with zero detectable flaws –
achieving technical perfection within the established style, yet still not introducing a new language. At this
apex of pre-perfection:
- Impeccable performance and intonation: Every note, whether played or sung, is in control. If the piece
involves live instruments, their tone and tuning are spot-on; any microtonal or expressive intonation
choices are clearly intentional. The dynamics of the performance (attack, sustain, release of notes) perfectly
match the piece’s emotional curve. For vocals (if present), breath control and phrasing are masterful – the
singer can convey intimacy without going off pitch or force when projecting power. In a purely electronic
piece, the timing of each event is deliberate and clear (no unintended sloppy timing or phase issues).
- Micro-timing and flow: The groove or temporal feel is exactly as intended. In a piece with a pulse, any
push-and-pull (rubato or laid-back groove) is executed consistently, not as an accident. If the music is free-
flowing (ambient drones or rubato piano), the timing of changes still feels natural and inevitable, never
arbitrary. The result is that the listener subconsciously trusts the music – it never feels “off” or uncertain for
even a moment.
- Harmonic and contrapuntal clarity: All harmonic intersections are clean. Dissonances appear only where
meant to, and they resolve or persist in a way that sounds exquisite rather than jarring. Counter-melodies
and accompaniment lines interlock without clashing; the voice-leading is smooth. For example, if a string
quartet plays four independent lines, they form rich chords and suspensions that melt appropriately – no
awkward notes stick out. The harmonic rhythm (the rate of chord change) supports the emotional beats
perfectly, accelerating or slowing at just the right moments to guide feeling.
- Mix and sonic architecture: The production values are top-notch. The mix translates well to any listening
environment (headphones, speakers, mono, etc.) – a sign of careful balance. Each instrument or layer has
its own space in the mix; frequency masking is minimized so that clarity is maintained even in dense
passages. Spatially, reverbs and delays are used in a deliberate way to either place the instruments in a
realistic room or an imaginatively designed space, and that spatial character is maintained consistently. If
the piece is supposed to be intimate, it remains intimate (no odd reverb tail suddenly making it cavernous
unless intended for a climax). Technical issues like background noise, hiss, or pops are either absent or used
creatively (for example, a vinyl crackle might be present but at a deliberate level to add atmosphere, not
because of poor recording).
- Aesthetic coherence: Every aspect of the piece – its sound, its composition, its title or any conceptual
packaging – aligns to a single artistic vision. There is no “genre-drag” element, meaning nothing included
just because convention says so. (E.g., the composer didn’t throw in a synth pad just because ambient tracks
usually have one; if it’s there, it’s truly serving the piece’s aesthetic.) This coherence often means the piece
evokes a strong, singular mood or imagery and sticks to it.
- Checks: CDPD ≈ 0.45–0.50 (even at perfection within known grammar, the piece might show moderate
pattern depth – it deviates somewhat from cookie-cutter patterns, but as a result of polish rather than
seeking originality); HMII = 4 (peak interplay of ~4 lines, indicating the piece can handle fairly complex
layering with complete clarity). There are zero structural or technical faults detectable by seasoned
listeners or professionals. If analyzed with the Sorelian metrics, it would score full marks on all execution
aspects. However, originality metrics would still indicate it’s using known grammar (so CDPD and NGE
plateau around the mid-range values noted).
Example: Arvo Pärt – “Spiegel im Spiegel” as performed in its definitive recordings could be seen here. It’s
a piece of utter simplicity and beauty, executed to perfection: every note of the piano and violin is placed
with absolute intention, the tuning and timing are immaculate, and the piece holds a singular mood of
6peace. It introduces no new harmonic language (simple triads and scale figures), but one cannot find a flaw
in its realization. Another example: high-end audiophile releases of ambient music where every texture is
perfectly sculpted (for instance, a Brian Eno piece in which each synth pad fades in and out at just the right
pace, every frequency balance is carefully tuned, and the noise floor is so controlled that the silence within
the music becomes palpable). At this tier, the work is faultless craft – a gold standard for its genre’s
technique, yet still operating inside a familiar linguistic framework.
Rungs 11–20: Post-Honinbo (New Grammar Emerges)
Starting at Rung 11, the ladder moves beyond flawless execution of known forms into the realm of
innovation and new grammar 4
. These works maintain the strengths of earlier tiers (coherence, craft,
beauty) while introducing ruptures or evolutions that expand the language of the music. Each step upward
from 11 to 20 demands deeper recursion, more profound motivic architecture, and bolder development of
internal logic. By Rung 20, the piece stands as an unprecedented event in its genre – with multiple
independent innovations and all aspects near perfection.
11 — First Rupture
Design test: The piece bends one fundamental rule of its genre and allows that bend to define its identity,
while still remaining recognizably within a quiet/cohesive style. In other words, this is the first tier where the
listener says, “I haven’t quite heard that done in this kind of music before,” yet the piece still feels like a
natural part of the genre. Key features:
- Single governing twist: There is one clear unconventional device introduced early and developed
throughout. It could be an odd-meter measure or rhythm embedded in an otherwise stable flow (e.g. a 5/4
or 7/8 bar appearing regularly in a 4/4 piece, giving a gentle lurch), a harmonic feint (like a chord that
sounds as if from another scale that keeps surfacing as a motif), an unusual timbre or sound-design layer
not typically central in the genre (perhaps a crackling noise, a whispered spoken word, or a prepared-piano
string pluck) or a structural fake-out (for example, a false ending or an unexpected pause that confounds
the usual build-release pattern). Importantly, this twist is intentional and audible – the listener can
perceive a “strange element” woven into the piece’s fabric.
- Early seeding: The twist is introduced by around the first major section of the piece. If the piece has a
notional “first chorus or climax,” the twist has appeared by then. This ensures the twist becomes part of the
piece’s personality, not just a one-off surprise at the end. Listeners begin to associate it with the track’s
identity.
- Structural role: The twist isn’t just a gimmick – it plays a role in marking or transitioning sections. For
example, every time the music moves to a new section or a higher intensity, the odd element is involved:
the odd meter bar might herald the transition into the climax each time, or the unusual sound (say a sudden
intake of breath or a tap on the body of the instrument) might appear as a cue signaling the next section.
Thus the twist element becomes a structural signpost or connective tissue in the form.
- Adaptation of other elements: The rest of the music responds to the twist when it occurs. If there’s a
brief shift in meter or rhythm, the melody or accompaniment might adjust its phrasing to complement that
shift (e.g. a piano motif might truncate a beat to flow naturally through a 7/8 bar). If the twist is a sonic one
(like a moment of filtered, lo-fi sound), the dynamics or instrumentation around it change to make space for
it. The key is that the twist is integrated: the piece “acknowledges” the rupture and works it in, rather than
just tolerating an out-of-place oddity.
- Checks: CDPD ≥ 0.60 (the introduction of a new rule and its integration means the piece has significantly
7stepped away from template reliance – more than half of it is now driven by its own novel continuity); NGE
≈ 0.50 (novelty jumps appreciably with the twist device, though the piece still has plenty of familiar
ground); HMII = 4 (the peak textural complexity often grows a bit at this tier – e.g. the twist might add an
extra independent line or effect to juggle, and overall interplay at climaxes involves about 4 distinct parts).
Additionally, the twist device itself should occupy a substantial portion of the runtime (audible ≥20% of the
piece’s duration) so it’s not just a trivial accent .
Example: Consider Sigur Rós’s tendency to insert a bar of unusual meter in otherwise flowing songs (this
is hypothetical but reflective of their spirit). A track might mostly be in a lulling 4/4, but every phrase ends
with one 2/4 bar, causing a gentle stutter – introduced in the first minute and repeated as a motif. The piece
remains ethereal post-rock, but that rhythmic hiccup becomes its quirk. Another example: Agnes Obel’s
“Stretch Your Eyes” features an unusual droning cello effect and a subtly off-kilter loop that recurs; it’s
seeded early and gives the song a haunting, otherworldly identity without alienating the listener. At this
rung, the track bends one rule and lets that bend define it, while still clearly belonging to its genre’s universe of
sound.
12 — Recursion Threshold
Design test: The piece achieves a self-referential motivic recursion: it folds back on itself, using one idea at
multiple scales so that the structure becomes a kind of fractal derived from a core motif. Rather than relying
on a typical verse-chorus or linear build, one musical cell generates the entire form through repetition
and variation on different levels. Key features:
- Core motif as seed: Identify a single short musical idea – it could be a melodic cell (a few notes with a
distinctive interval pattern), a rhythmic pattern (a particular syncopation or cadence of notes/rests), or
even a timbre/texture motif (say, a specific way a chord swells with a crescendo-decrescendo). This motif
becomes the seed that will appear in various guises.
- Multi-scale use: The core motif manifests on at least three different structural levels:
- Micro (bar-level): For example, the motif might be a 4-note figure that appears at the end of a phrase as a
little hook or in a piano accompaniment riff.
- Meso (phrase or section-level): The shape of the motif could determine a longer melody or bassline. For
instance, the entire chorus theme might be a stretched or sequenced version of that 4-note figure, or an
oscillating dynamic pattern in a long ambient section might mirror the motif’s rhythmic contour.
- Macro (section or whole-form-level): The motif’s pattern might inform the structure of sections themselves.
Perhaps the piece has alternating loud and soft sections following the motif’s short-long pattern, or the
number of repeats of sections is itself drawn from the motif (e.g. a motif with three notes leads to three
verses or three climactic swells).
- Motivic echoes across layers: The piece might literally echo the motif in different registers or instruments
(e.g. a high glockenspiel plays a tiny fragment that later the cello plays slower as a bass line). There is a
sense of self-similarity: listening carefully, one realizes the big melody and that little background figure share
a shape, and maybe the rhythmic hits of the percussion also align with it.
- Production recursion: Even the production and effects join in the game. For example, if the motif is
rhythmic, maybe a delay effect is timed to that rhythm and creates repeats that reinforce it. Or a subtle
filter LFO might pulse at a rate related to the motif’s tempo, echoing the motif’s presence subliminally.
Another trick: a reverb tail might contain a faint repetition of the motif’s notes (perhaps via convolution or a
creative send), so the space itself reiterates the motif.
- (If vocals or text are present) Lyric/text recursion: The lyrics might repeat a key phrase with small
variations in meaning each time (for example, flipping a pronoun or tense, or reordering words) while
8keeping the same rhythmic shape. This way the concept also circles back on itself. In instrumental music,
one could analogously have a titular or programmatic element recur (like a theme that represents “dawn”
appears in different contexts throughout a narrative piece about a day’s cycle).
- Checks: NGE ≈ 0.55 (the novelty is fairly high here because structuring an entire piece from a self-
referential motif is an advanced, less common technique – the piece distinguishes itself by this recursive
design); HMII = 4–5 (to pull off multi-level recursion, typically multiple layers must be active and interlocking
– often a rich counterpoint emerges, with perhaps up to 5 voices echoing or building on the motif). The
motif or its variants should be present in ≥3 distinct strata – melody, accompaniment, bass, rhythmic
pattern, or textural pattern .
Example: Arvo Pärt – “Fratres” exhibits this kind of recursion. A single chord progression and motif is
explored through several variations (fast figures, slow chords, different instrumentation), effectively
generating the piece’s entire form from one idea. In a more contemporary ambient vein, Hania Rani’s
looping piano pieces often take a small riff and repeat it at various speeds and registers – the same
pattern might be the arpeggiated accompaniment in one section and, slowed down, the main theme in
another. The result is a piece that seems to constantly reference itself, creating a satisfying unity. At this rung,
the song uses one idea as a self-mirroring structure rather than a disposable hook, achieving a new level of
integrative architecture.
13 — Inhabited Form
Design test: The piece establishes such a cohesive sonic world that it feels less like a sequence of
measures and more like a place or environment the listener visits. Every aspect of the mix and arrangement
contributes to a tangible “sonic habitat.” The form is inhabited by distinct roles and spaces that persist and
evolve, giving the impression of a consistent musical environment one can step into. Key features:
- Sonic habitat: From the first moments, the piece defines a particular atmospheric setting and maintains
it. This could be achieved through consistent use of ambience (e.g. a specific reverb that places everything
in a imagined room or cavern), a particular textural bed (like tape hiss or vinyl crackle underlying the track,
or a nature sound like wind that persists), or an instrumental palette that remains focused (say, only a string
trio and harp, recorded closely so you “live” in that chamber with them). As the piece progresses, this
habitat might evolve (e.g. more reverb added in chorus to expand the space, or elements of the texture
change like day turning to night) but it does not switch arbitrarily to totally new environments. It’s like the
setting of a story – it can deepen and shift lighting, but we’re in the same world throughout.
- Instrument roles and narrative jobs: Each instrument or layer in the arrangement has a clear role within
this habitat, almost as characters in a scene. For instance, one warm pad or drone might consistently act as
the “ground” or pulse of the room (providing sonic glue or a sense of space’s hum), a piano might act as
the narrator delivering the main motifs, a faint violin line might come and go as a highlight or shadow
accent. These roles are adhered to – the pad doesn’t suddenly take the melody, the piano doesn’t randomly
switch to percussive noise – unless such a change is itself a deliberate evolution of the narrative. Because of
this consistency, one could describe what each element does in the piece (“the low cello is always the dark
undercurrent, the bell tree sparkles introduce each new section,” etc.). The ensemble behaves like an
ecosystem with each element in an ecological niche.
- Register and space mapping: The allocation of frequency/register and stereo or depth space is
intentional and often section-dependent. For example, in quieter sections the higher frequencies might be
sparse (creating a “low ceiling” intimate feel), and in the climax more high-end (cymbals or shimmer effects)
fill the air to make the space feel expansive. Likewise, panning and depth might change with form: verses
very narrow and centered (like a small room), choruses wide and panoramic. These spatial moves are tied to
9section purpose – not gimmicks. A listener might not consciously note “the reverb got bigger in the chorus”
but will feel that the space opened up when the emotional message opened up.
- State shifts without habitat break: Each major section or repeat in the form brings a clear change in
state – for example, the second verse introduces new background textures and slight tempo push, or each
chorus adds more layers to sound more “populated” – yet these changes feel like natural transformations
of the same world. It’s as if the piece shows you different rooms of the same building, rather than
teleporting you to a new building. A chorus might feel like the same theme now in a larger hall, or the final
section might feel like the initial room now empty and echoing. Listeners (even blindfolded to harmonic content) could group sections by the “place” they evoke .
- Checks: NGE ≈ 0.55–0.60 (the novelty here comes from the holistic cohesion – fewer pieces achieve this
kind of environmental unity, so it stands out, though it might not introduce shockingly new chords or
rhythms); HMII = 4–5 (with distinct roles interacting, the texture can be rich – up to five layers might be
present, each intelligible in the mix). A blindfold test listener should be able to tell that all parts of the track
belong to the same “world” even if chord progressions change – that’s a sign of an inhabited form. The piece feels like a location you return to, rather than just a file that starts and stops .
Example: Grouper’s recordings (e.g. the album Ruins, track “Labyrinth”) exemplify this inhabited quality. The
faint room noise, the distant thunder and croaking frogs in the background, the consistent intimate reverb
on her voice and piano – it all puts the listener in a specific cabin on a rainy night, a world she maintains
throughout the song. Each instrument (voice, piano, field recording) has a clear role in painting that
atmosphere. Another example: Sigur Rós – “Vaka” (Untitled #1), where the band creates a soft, snowy
sound-world – the gentle organ drone is the ever-present sky, the music-box piano motif is like a figure
walking through, and the bowed guitar textures occasionally swell like gusts of wind. Section to section, the
intensity shifts, but the sonic identity remains so coherent that you feel you could live inside that sound. At
rung 13, the track’s mix and arrangement define a recognizable world for the listener – a place they can
inhabit in imagination.
14 — Harmonic Duality
Design test: The piece sustains two independent musical tensions at once – essentially running dual
narratives in parallel – in a way that heightens emotional complexity while remarkably preserving clarity and
beauty. This often manifests as a harmonic or rhythmic ambivalence: the music lets contradictory impulses
coexist for an extended period, creating a poignant “pull” in multiple directions, yet the piece never
collapses into chaos. Key features:
- Dual harmonic tension (sweet vs. shadow): Commonly, one narrative is harmonic. For example, the
piece might present a graceful, consonant melody (something you could hum easily) against an
undercurrent of unusual or destabilizing harmony. Imagine a lullaby-like theme floating over chords that
imply a different mode or key (e.g. a melody clearly in C major while the accompaniment hints at E♭ major
or an ambiguous modal mixture). The ear can follow the pretty melody, but it simultaneously senses
another harmonic gravity at work. Alternatively, the harmony may be largely consonant but contains a
persistent dissonant pedal or a chord that doesn’t resolve, instilling a delicious unresolved tension.
- Dual rhythmic or textural tension (stability vs. cross-current): If not harmonic, the second narrative
can be rhythmic/textural. For instance, a piece in an ostensibly steady meter (say a calm 4/4 pulse) might
have a secondary pattern that contradicts it – like a piano playing triplet arpeggios that cycle with a 5-beat
pattern across the 4/4, or a secondary instrument accenting off-beats in a way that creates a slow
polyrhythm. The effect is the music feels grounded yet off-kilter at the same time. The listener might tap their
foot to the gentle primary pulse, but there’s a cross-rhythm that tugs at that stability, creating an internal
10tension.
- Span: The piece holds these dual tensions for an extended duration, not just a fleeting effect. At least a
full major section (on the order of a verse+chorus length, or a multi-minute span in ambient context)
sustains the overlap of both narratives. This prolonged exposure to a musical “paradox” is what elevates the
emotional complexity – the listener sits in a bittersweet or unsettled state where the music is pulling them
in two directions (e.g., comforting and haunting, or serene and restless simultaneously).
- Emotional clarity despite complexity: Amazingly, even with two layers of logic, the piece remains legible
and emotionally powerful. A casual listener might not intellectually parse the two separate threads, but they
“feel something different” – a sense that there’s more under the surface. Yet they can still hum the main
tune or sway with the main rhythm; the piece doesn’t alienate the non-analytical listener. Achieving this
requires great care in voice-leading and mix balance: the conflicting elements must be balanced such that
they enhance each other rather than obscuring. Dissonances or polyrhythms are positioned (through
careful timing, register, and dynamics) so that they add color and energy without turning the sound to
mush. The bass or fundamental layer often provides an anchor (e.g., a pedal tone or consistent pattern) to give the ear a home base while the two tensions play out above .
- Voice-leading & blending: All potentially clashing elements are woven with clear voice-leading. For
harmonic duality, any dissonant notes (extensions, suspensions) are treated carefully – they resolve or move
in a way that the ear can follow, and dissonances are spaced (not too tight or in the wrong register) to avoid
unpleasant clashing. If rhythmic, the cross-rhythm is introduced gradually or in a complementary register
so that it doesn’t just sound like a mistake. The result is a lush harmonic tapestry or rhythmic braid where
each thread is distinct but the combination is strangely harmonious.
- Checks: CDPD ≈ 0.70 (the willingness to hold two conflicting ideas signals a strong break from simple
template music – the piece asserts its own complex grammar, distancing significantly from generic
conventions); NGE ≈ 0.60 (this kind of duality is relatively novel – not many pieces sustain it gracefully, so
novelty is high); HMII ≥ 5 (to juggle multiple independent tensions, typically there are at least 5 voices or
layers interacting: e.g., melody, bass, and one or two harmonic lines plus a textural line all doing slightly
different things). If one of the tensions is harmonic, TRX complexity (HMII) must be high – for instance, an
independent bass line and a counter-melody might each carry a different harmonic implication 14
. Despite
the complexity, the listener can still hum the main hook or recall the primary theme 15
– legibility remains.
Example: Sigur Rós – “Viðrar vel til loftárása” offers a subtle illustration. The song carries a gorgeous,
lyrical piano theme (emotional and consonant) while underneath, the string arrangement grows
increasingly atonal and restless towards the climax. For a lengthy section, you feel the sweeping, uplifting
melody and a creeping tension from the strings — two moods at once — culminating in an intensely
bittersweet feeling. Another example in modern composition: a piece where a simple chorale in the upper
voices is accompanied by an unrelated slow-moving counter-melody in the cellos in a clashing key – if
balanced right, it creates a new, haunting harmony rather than chaos. At rung 14, such conflicting
harmonic/rhythmic pulls raise the emotional charge without losing the music’s legibility 16
. The
listener might not know why it feels so emotionally rich until they realize two stories were being told
together.
15 — Divine Injury
Design test: The piece undergoes a singular, profound rupture event – a wounding moment in the music so
stark that everything after carries a scar from it. This rung’s piece dares to “injure” its own beauty or flow in
a deliberate act of musical trauma, and then it lives in the aftermath of that event. Importantly, the
rupture, while jarring, is meaningfully integrated: it elevates the narrative rather than derailing it. Key
11features:
- The wound event: There is an identifiable moment (down to a particular bar or second) where “it
happened”. This could take many forms: a sudden drop into silence or near-silence (a stark breakdown
where almost everything cuts out unexpectedly), an abrupt dissonant or out-of-key chord that slashes
through an otherwise consonant progression, a brutal textural strip where lush instrumentation is
reduced to a single bare thread, a tempo or meter flip that’s shockingly discontinuous (e.g., the piece was
floating in free rhythm and suddenly snaps into a strict pulse, or vice versa), or even a foreign element
intrusion (like a snippet of a field recording, a spoken phrase, or electronic noise that appears out of
nowhere). Whatever it is, it violates the established norm of the piece dramatically – it’s the kind of moment
where even a casual listener would widen their eyes or hold their breath, instantly recognizing “something
changed just now.”
 
- Pre-echo foreshadowing: A masterful Divine Injury doesn’t come completely out of the blue; there are
foreshadowings earlier. Subtle hints of the coming break appear in the first half of the piece. For example, a
faint version of the disruptive element might be heard briefly (a quick drop to silence for a beat, a quiet
dissonant note in the background, a tempo hiccup that lasts a second and then corrects). Or perhaps a lyric
in an earlier verse metaphorically alludes to a “break” or “fall” to come (if applicable). These pre-echoes are
short and might go unnoticed on first listen, but in retrospect one sees they signaled the impending change
 
- The aftermath (post-echo): After the rupture, things are not the same. Some element that was present
before is now absent or fundamentally altered. For instance, if earlier the music always had a rich pad or
chorus of voices, after the injury that fullness never fully returns – perhaps the main motif comes back but
now thinly scored, missing that pad (the piece bears a hollow quality post-trauma). Or if the song was in a
major key, after a shocking modulation it stays in a different, more somber key and never goes back to the
original warmth. Maybe the lyrics after the event take on a different tone or perspective. The “pre-wound
habit” (like a comfortable repeating pattern or chord resolution) is gone or transformed 19
. This absence
is itself a poignant presence – the listener subconsciously yearns for the return of the old comfort, but the
music refuses, illustrating the concept of a scar.
- Rule change: Along with content differences, the piece establishes new rules or constraints after the
event. Perhaps the rhythms become more sparse and halting (if the wound was something like a big
silence, now the music is afraid to fill all the space). Or instrumentation might shift to a tighter range (as if
speaking in hushed tones after a shock). A clear example: if a loud, chaotic climax “injures” the piece at the
golden mean, everything after is subdued, minimal, or in a minor mode. The composition might impose
constraints like “no full chords, only single notes” after the break, symbolizing fragility. In essence, the latter
 sections obey a new law that came directly from the disruptive event .
- Emotional impact and integration: The event, while jarring, doesn’t feel random or gimmicky – it is
emotionally or thematically justified. The listener can sense that this is the climax/turning point of the
narrative. Often, the best “wound” moments are positioned around the 2/3 or 3/4 mark of the piece (similar
to a bridge in a song or the third act twist in a story), giving enough time after for aftermath. Listeners
should be able to point to the exact moment where “everything changed” 21
, and what follows, though
different, should still feel like it’s part of the same piece’s story (the scar belongs to the body, after all). If
done well, this is incredibly powerful: it can bring a sense of tragedy or catharsis that a smoothly unbroken
piece might not achieve.
- Checks: NGE ≈ 0.62–0.65 (quite high novelty – executing a deliberate rupture and new post-rupture
grammar is uncommon and creative); HMII ≥ 5 (the moment of rupture and possibly the buildup to it often
involve heightened complexity – for example, a massive textural build-up to a collapse likely has many
layers, or a sudden polyrhythmic break might involve multiple lines going out of phase). After the break, the
12complexity might drop, but the peak HMII remains high. The key qualitative check: listeners can point to the
exact bar or second where “it changed.”
 
Example: Sigur Rós – “Festival” provides a dramatic illustration. The song’s first half is slow, hymn-like,
gradually building. Then, there’s a moment of near silence – a brief pause pregnant with anticipation –
before the second half erupts with pounding drums and ecstatic vocals in a new, driving tempo. That pause
and tempo flip is the “wound” where the serene ritual of the first half is shattered, and the rest of the track
is on entirely new footing (faster, more urgent, and never returns to the old tempo or calmness). Another
example in a more classical mode: Arvo Pärt’s “Silentium” (second movement of Tabula Rasa) arrives only
after the violent end of the first movement – the piece seems to die off, and then we’re left with the second
movement’s sparse, fragile repetitions in the wake of that death. In both cases, one event forever changes
the music. At rung 15, one event injures the piece’s chassis, and everything after carries the scar of that injury, yielding an unforgettable emotional narrative.
16 — Impossible Originality
Design test: The piece’s design is so original and singular that it seems to create its own method or
musical “language” from scratch – one that could be repeated in theory, but is clearly unique to its creator. At
this rung, the work has a method fingerprint: a set of systematic compositional choices that are
unmistakably that artist’s own invention, audible to an expert listener as an invented system. The
achievement is that the track still works as music, not just an experiment. Key features:
- Method fingerprint: There is a clear, coherent method at work behind the music – almost like the
composer wrote a new rulebook for how to create this piece. For example, the artist might have a unique
tuning system (perhaps using microtones or just intonation in a consistent, novel way), a particular
rhythmic law (maybe every phrase increases by one beat in a pattern, or all rhythms avoid certain common
divisions, creating a distinct forward momentum), a motif construction law (e.g., all melodies are
palindromes, or derive from spelling a word in Morse code, etc.), or a particular layering technique (like
one instrument only ever plays in harmonic series overtones while another only plays fundamentals).
Whatever the specifics, these choices pervade the piece and give it a strong identity. An analyst could
attempt to write down “rules” that generate such a piece.
- Repeatability: The method is not just a one-off trick; it could be used to create more pieces (though one
might need the composer’s mind to do it well). In other words, the piece doesn’t feel random at all – it feels
as if it’s following some hidden logic that, once discovered, could be reapplied. Indeed, many times rung-16
level works spawn entire schools or imitations, because others can pick up aspects of the method. This is
the threshold of not just a unique piece, but the birth of a new mini-genre or technique.
- Distribution across the piece: The unique method governs at least 50% of the piece’s runtime and
structure 23
. It’s not a single clever section or isolated effect; it’s woven throughout the form. For instance,
if the method is a special way of voice-leading, you’ll hear it in every section in some form. If it’s a novel
rhythmic system, it might relax in a bridge but is present in most of the piece’s sections. This ensures the
piece sounds consistently unique, not just “normal music plus one weird part.”
- Axis alignment: All major aspects of the music align to this individuality. The melodic content, the
harmonic progressions (if any), the lyrics or themes (if present), the production choices – they all seem to
serve this one unified method or concept rather than defaulting to generic behavior in one dimension. For
instance, if the method is about layering loops of different lengths (à la Steve Reich but taken further), then
maybe the production emphasizes the slight phase shifts (with panning or filters highlighting them) and the
harmony arises from those loops rather than standard chord changes. Nothing in the piece feels like a template or a trend-chasing insert; everything is bespoke to the piece’s new logic .
13- Audible uniqueness: An expert listener, or even an attentive casual listener, could blindly identify the
artist or project behind the piece because no one else makes music that sounds like this. At very least, they
identify it as highly distinctive. This is where we often say “only X could have made this.” The techniques and
the sonic result are that characteristic. For example, the first time listeners encountered Arvo Pärt’s
tintinnabuli compositions, it was clear that this was an entirely new sound – now we can identify a Pärt
piece by ear because of that method. Similarly, certain electronic producers have such singular sound
design and composition rules that their tracks are immediately recognizable.
- Checks: CDPD ≥ 0.80 (the piece is almost entirely distant from generic templates – it has forged its own
continuity and patterns, borrowing little if anything from convention); NGE ≈ 0.65 (very high novelty – it
introduces a palpable new grammar); HMII ≥ 5 (often with original methods, complexity is present because
the composer is managing multiple layers of innovation – at least five interwoven parts might be doing
distinct things according to the new rules). An expert listener could hear this and know who made it or at
least recognize the singular technique 25
. In Sorelian terms, this corresponds to an axis score of originality so high that the work could only come from one creative fingerprint .
Example: Arvo Pärt’s “Für Alina” (1976) – the piece that introduced his tintinnabuli technique – is a classic
case of impossible originality. The composition follows a strict, idiosyncratic rule (one voice moves stepwise,
the other plays notes of the triad according to a pattern) that Pärt invented; it sounded like nothing before
it. The method is clear and repeatable (he went on to write many pieces with it), and any knowledgeable
listener can identify a tintinnabuli piece. Another example: Sigur Rós’s signature style circa Ágætis byrjun
– the bowed guitar, Hopelandic vocalizations, slow-building orchestration – was so unique at the time that it
essentially carved out a new subgenre of post-rock. It’s immediately recognizable and stemmed from their
particular approach to songwriting and sound (one could describe their method: sustained bowed tones,
reverb-soaked falsetto singing without lyrical language, etc.). At rung 16, the track could only plausibly
come from one creative source 26
, and that source has essentially pioneered a new method that others
can study or emulate.
17 — Sublime Disintegration
Design test: The piece deliberately disintegrates its own form in a controlled, artful way, producing an
experience of sublime fragmentation. Starting from a stable, coherent musical form, it begins to shear and
fragment as it progresses – elements overlap, structures fissure, fragments of motifs reappear out of sync
– yet the process is guided so carefully that the result is fascinating instead of confusing. Crucially, a
narrative spine or anchor remains, preventing total chaos. This rung is about dissolving form without losing
meaning. Key features:
- Controlled fracture trajectory: The piece is structured to undergo stages of breakdown. It often looks
like:
1. Stable form – The music starts in a normal, perhaps even very conventional, state (for contrast and set-
up). It could be a verse-chorus loop, a clear theme and accompaniment, etc. The listener gets a footing.
2. Hairline cracks – Subtle oddities begin. Maybe an echo repeats a bit too long, a background vocal comes
in slightly misaligned, a chord sustains dissonantly into the next, or previous sections’ motifs ghost quietly
under the current section. These are like cracks appearing but not disrupting the structure yet .
3. Shard stage (full fragmentation): At a certain peak, the music enters a passage where structure as we
knew it breaks down. Multiple motifs or sections might start overlapping or colliding. For example, the
chorus melody might be layered on top of verse chords simultaneously, or fragments of different earlier
moments all play at once in a collage. Rhythmically, things might desynchronize – polyrhythms or tempo
smears, time stretching, or abrupt alternations give a sense of being unmoored. There might be deliberate
14metric blurs (the beat becomes obscured) or harmonic kaleidoscope effects (rapidly cycling tonal centers
or layering different keys) 28
. It can be intense or chaotic, but it’s intentionally composed chaos.
4. Partial reintegration or exhaustion: After the peak fragmentation, the piece doesn’t simply snap back
to normal (that would undermine the journey). Instead, it might attempt a partial reintegration – perhaps a
final section where a semblance of the original theme returns, but in a bare or transfigured way, as if
piecing itself back together differently. Alternatively, the piece might end in an exhausted fade-out, where
the fragments dissipate one by one, leaving just an echo or a single element (like a lone instrument
repeating a motif quietly, everything else having fallen away). In either case, we don’t return to full stability;
we end with the memory of the disintegration as part of the form.
- Anchoring element: Amidst the fragmentation, there is an anchor that prevents the music from feeling
like pure noise. Often this is a fixed element that persists: a steady low drone or pedal note humming
throughout, or a constant rhythmic pulse (even if muffled or faint) that continues under the chaos. It could
even be a consistent timbral aspect, like the tape hiss or room tone that remains unbroken. This element
acts like a reference point in the madness, a thread the listener can hold onto so they know the piece itself
hasn’t disappeared – it’s intentionally walking into the storm and out. In doing so, it creates the sublime
feeling: one witnesses destruction from the safety of that anchor.
- Vocal/instrumental strategy (if applicable): If there is a lead voice (instrumental or vocal), its behavior
helps articulate the disintegration. For instance, a vocalist might sing with pristine clarity on certain lines
(especially early on or on key lyrics), but then intentionally smears or overlaps phrases later – singing over
herself via loops or multi-tracking, or allowing words to stretch and blur. At times the voice might become
texture (breathy hums, glossolalia) in the chaotic sections, then perhaps resume clarity for a final line. This
alternation between crystal clear statements and obscured, layered ones heightens the sense of something
falling apart and partially re-forming .
- Cohesive narrative arc: Even though the form breaks, the emotional or narrative arc is coherent. A focused
listener should, after one listen, be able to recount the journey: “It started calm, then things got strange
with echoes of old themes, then everything was colliding and I felt lost, and finally it ended with a solitary
reprise that felt like looking at the pieces left…” 31
. In other words, the disintegration serves an expressive
narrative, not just an abstract experiment. By the end, the listener should feel they went through something
purposeful.
- Checks: NGE ≈ 0.66–0.68 (very high novelty – deliberate form disintegration is rare and creative; it’s
pushing the grammar to its limits); HMII ≥ 5 (in the shard stage, complexity likely peaks beyond 5
independent layers – indeed multiple themes may overlap, creating a dense polyphony of fragments). The
qualitative success criterion: after one listen, a focused listener can retell the journey 31
– meaning the
piece didn’t lose them completely; it leaves a strong impression of the transformation it enacted.
Example: Sigur Rós – “Untitled #8 (Popplagið)” can be interpreted this way. It begins as a fairly structured
song with verses and choruses. As it progresses, the band layers on an enormous crescendo of noise –
feedback, drums, bass, all motifs piling into a chaotic wall. Through it, the basic chord cycle (an anchor)
continues insistently on the bass. The vocals, which were clear, become screams and then wordless cries
drowned in sound. By the end, the structure has disintegrated into furious noise and then cuts off, leaving a
stunned silence. Listeners can trace that journey from order to sublime chaos. Another case: William
Basinski’s “Disintegration Loops” (though an entire concept album rather than a single piece of normal
length) – taped melodies that literally crumble over time, repeating until they turn into smeared ghosts of
themselves, with the tape hiss as an anchor and the process itself as the art. In all such works, the music
partially dissolves, but the narrative spine – however thin by the end – stays intact 32
, resulting in a haunting,
profound experience.

Design test: The piece transcends the typical single-arc structure and instead puts the listener through a
multi-phase odyssey – at least three distinct musical states or chapters – each with its own character and

- Multi-state arc: The composition delineates three or more distinct sections that differ significantly in

aggressive), and (4) a hollow coda (very minimal, atmospheric, reflective). These states are not slight
variations – each is like entering a new realm, clearly separated in how they feel and sound .
- Gateways (transformational transitions): The transitions between these major states are events in
themselves – not smooth, gradual crossfades, but rites of passage. They might be heralded by a sudden
silence, a bold key change, a tempo shift, or a dramatic sound effect (like a swell of noise or a cascading
harp glissando) – something that marks a clear turning point. These transitions feel like gateways one steps
through: you know unequivocally that the old section has ended and a new one has begun. It’s not an edit
or medley of unrelated pieces, however; the transitions, while dramatic, have an inner logic (perhaps the
final chord of one section is revoiced as the first chord of the next in a different tempo, etc.) .
- Dual (or multiple) thematic continuity: Despite the stark differences between sections, there are threads
that persist through all states, tying the journey together. Typically: one sonic element and one motivic
element survive across all states, though in evolving forms 36
. For instance, a certain instrumental timbre
(say, a specific synth tone or a particular piano motif) might appear in each section, adapted to fit (it might
be gentle in the first, triumphant in the second, distorted in the third). And a textual or melodic motif
might carry through – if there are lyrics, perhaps a key phrase or lyrical theme recurs in each section with
different words; if instrumental, a short melody or chord progression reappears in each new state,
transformed. This dual continuity ensures the piece feels like one story rather than three random pieces
glued together.
- Sustained sections: Each state holds a substantial duration on its own – on the order of a minute or more,
at least (or roughly 32+ bars if counted) 37
. There are no throwaway segments; even an intro must be a
meaningful first state, not just 10 seconds of ambience. Each section deepens the emotional narrative and
is long enough for the listener to fully inhabit that space before being thrust to the next. This is partly what
makes it an “ordeal” – you truly experience each phase.
- Legibility of the journey: After experiencing the piece, listeners can narrate the progression (e.g., “It began
calm and hopeful, then there was this intense battle-like part, and finally it ended in a mournful, empty
soundscape”). The emotions of each state are clear enough to identify, and the overall emotional trajectory
is perceivable (be it from light to darkness to light again, or innocence to experience to transcendence, etc.).
The piece feels like it means something, like each state was a necessary chapter. In many cases, this kind of
piece has a conceptual or programmatic element – the composer might be depicting something like stages
of grief, movements of a symphony, or scenes in a story – but even without an explicit program, the
structure implies a narrative.

19 — Forbidden Tone
Design test: The piece takes an element that is normally “forbidden” or avoided in its genre – something
that by convention would be considered too disruptive, ugly, or out-of-place – and makes it the core of its
sonic language, all while miraculously maintaining the music’s integrity and emotional appeal. In the quiet,
harmonic-motivic context, this often means introducing an extreme of timbre or sparsity that would
typically break the spell of beauty, yet here it becomes a new kind of beauty. Key features:
- Taboo element as main grammar: Identify a production or compositional choice that quiet, spatially-
poised music usually keeps at the margins. This could be an extremely raw or noisy texture (e.g., tape
hiss, amplifier hum, feedback, or distortion that one would normally dial out), a harsh dissonance or
microtonal interval that normally would clash with the genre’s consonant ethos, an ultra-minimalist
approach (e.g., stretches of near-silence or single-note ostinati that risk boredom or “nothing happening”),
or some odd performance technique (like heavy bow pressure on strings causing crunch, or key clicks on a
piano). At rung 19, the piece not only uses this “forbidden” element, it makes it the central language of
the piece. For instance, the entire track might revolve around layers of ambient tape hiss swells and distant
melody (turning noise into music), or be built on a single atonal chord that is textured and explored
endlessly, or hold extensive silences that are as important as notes.
- Legibility and discipline: Despite the unconventional material, the piece remains coherent and
emotionally intelligible. The main motifs (be they melodic fragments, chord progressions, or textural
gestures) are still discernible and compelling within that edgy sonic palette. For example, if using heavy
distortion, the melody is perhaps carried by it but is crafted such that one can still follow its contour; if using
microtones or dissonant clusters, they might be voiced in a way that creates a haunting harmony rather
than random dissonance. The listener might initially find the timbre challenging, but soon perceives
structure and pattern in it. Essentially, the compositional discipline is extremely high to counterbalance the
risk of the forbidden element. The piece never descends into actual chaos or nonsense – it harnesses the
“wrong” sound in a controlled, expressive manner .
- Span: The forbidden tone or device governs at least half of the piece’s runtime, including at least one of
its climactic or primary sections 40
. This isn’t a brief gimmick or intro effect; it’s sustained. The composer
commits to it. If it’s distortion, the climaxes are fully distorted; if it’s silence, large portions of the piece hinge
on silence or extremely sparse texture; if it’s extreme dissonance, then entire sections keep that tension.
The audience is asked to live in this unusual sound world for a significant duration, not just taste it.
- Integration with performance and expression: The performers (or the compositional execution in
17electronic cases) embrace the device. If the piece is largely near-silent, the performers modulate their
dynamics with incredible control and intention, turning silence into part of the rhythm and phrasing. If it’s
microtonal or dissonant, the performers lean into those notes with conviction and perhaps adjust their
timbres to make them speak (like singers adjusting vowel shapes to make dissonances sound resonant).
The lyrics or emotional performance (if any) might even acknowledge or align with the strangeness – e.g.,
singing with a brittle, close-mic intimacy if the production is very lo-fi and noisy, thereby using the noise
expressively instead of fighting it. Nothing feels like “oh we just slapped an effect on”; it feels like the whole
ensemble or composition is built around this new language .
- Gates and technicals: To reach rung 19, all the prior originality and continuity gates are pushed to
extreme high: CDPD ≥ 0.85 (the piece has virtually no reliance on common template – it’s in its own
domain), NGE (median) ≥ 0.60 (even when it’s not at its most novel moment, it sustains high novelty; peak
novelty might be higher), and HMII ≥ 5 (despite maybe a sparse aesthetic, the underlying interplay or the
conceptual complexity rates high; for instance, precisely balancing silence and sound among players
demands polyphonic awareness). Additionally, the piece must show high marks in axes like Structural
Coherence and Beauty even under these conditions – e.g., BP (Beauty & Spatial Poise) ≥ 16 on a 20 scale
. This ensures that, though it uses a “forbidden” element, the result is still deeply moving or captivating,
not alienating.
- Design test in summary: A “wrong” sound becomes the primary language of the piece, yet the piece
remains unmistakably gorgeous or powerful in its quiet way 43
. It’s a high-wire act of turning a flaw into a
feature, and succeeding spectacularly.
Example: Grouper’s ultra-lofi recordings can illustrate this. On some tracks, Liz Harris records on a 4-track
cassette with a wash of hiss and room noise, her voice obscured – something most producers would “clean
up” – but instead that haze becomes the soul of the music, enveloping the gentle melodies. What should
make the song unlistenable (thick hiss, indistinct vocals) actually makes it otherworldly and intimate.
Another example: Arvo Pärt’s Spiegel im Spiegel might be cited for extreme simplicity (though not
“forbidden”, its daring minimalism was unusual), but more fitting would be a hypothetical piece where Pärt
uses outright silence as a structural element – say, measures of silence interwoven with sparse notes, making
silence as salient as sound (John Cage’s 4’33” is an extreme conceptual version – pure silence as music –
which was taboo turned into statement). At rung 19, think of a piece that might consist largely of what
others consider “not music” – yet by the end, the audience is deeply moved and finds it undeniably music. It
brings a fringe device to the center without losing the listener. The track reads unmistakably as a valid,
even profound entry in its genre despite using a normally “wrong” sound as its language.
20 — Apocryphal Event Horizon
Design test: The piece reaches the absolute pinnacle of individual innovation: it successfully combines two
entirely independent, orthogonal innovations such that each alone could have carried a piece to high
originality, but here they coexist and define a brand new frontier 44
. In addition, it meets every technical
and artistic benchmark of perfection – it’s an event horizon beyond which the genre’s conventional
understanding breaks down (apocryphal in the sense of beyond accepted canon). Key features:
- Two orthogonal innovations: There are two distinct innovative ideas at play, each in a different domain
of music creation, and each so robust that either one could have been the focal innovation of a very original
piece. One tends to be more procedural/structural, the other more sonic/timbral/formal 45
. For
example:
- A procedural innovation might be a new compositional system (a new way of generating rhythms, a novel
tuning system, a recursive form algorithm, generative techniques, interactive composition, etc.) or
18something like an unprecedented structural framework (e.g., music that follows a mathematical sequence
at multiple scales, or a piece that evolves its own rules as it plays – essentially a new grammar or method).
- A sonic/formal innovation might be a revolutionary use of technology or timbre (say, using biofeedback
from plants to control music, or inventing a new instrument or sound synthesis method), or a new approach
to performance (like ensemble spatialization, audience participation element), or a formal presentation
twist (music that is meant to be heard both forwards and backwards for example).
Each innovation is strong enough to support an entire track alone 46
. Meaning, if you “turned off” one
and kept the other, you’d still have a piece that could rank perhaps in the mid-high teens of this ladder.
- Orthogonality (independent axes): The two innovations operate largely independently – they affect
different dimensions of the music such that they do not interfere or overlap in a reductive way. For instance,
one might govern pitch/harmony structure while the other governs rhythmic/spatial presentation. This
orthogonality is critical: it ensures that the piece isn’t just one idea with a slight variation, but genuinely two
novel ideas in interplay. Removing either innovation would still leave a distinct, coherent piece (albeit less
groundbreaking), and importantly, each removed innovation would leave the piece in a different place. It’s
as if the track is the intersection of two new subspaces – take one away, and you’re in one unique subspace;
take the other away, you’re in another 47
. Together, they define a whole new territory that neither alone
could fully chart.
- Combined span: These two innovations co-carry at least 50% of the runtime 47
. Both are integral to
the piece’s core sections (especially around the central “hinge” or peak of the piece). They aren’t confined to
separate sections (like one innovation in the first half, the other in second half, which would be two short
separate novelties rather than a synergistic novelty). Instead, they intertwine – you hear them functioning
together, creating effects that neither alone could. The piece likely has a central section or overarching form
where both innovations are in full effect simultaneously.
- Documented method: The methods or principles behind these innovations are conscious and
methodical – they can be explained or notated to a degree. The piece’s creators could write down how the
piece works, maybe not in a simple traditional score, but in a clear design spec of laws, algorithms, or
procedures. It’s not an accidental stroke of genius; it’s an intentional, repeatable method bundle (even if only
the inventor can practically execute it at first) 48
. This is implied by “event horizon” – it’s not a one-off
anomaly, it’s a portal to new reproducible possibilities.
- Comprehensive excellence: On top of the double innovation, the piece suffers no compromise in other
areas. It meets all the Tier-20 gate criteria of Sorelian evaluation: CDPD ≥ 0.85; NGE ≥ 0.70 (truly high
novelty); HMII ≥ 5 (complex interplay at least at peaks); every secondary axis (SC, MI, BP, EG, CD as we
defined) is essentially maxed out or nearly so (each sub-factor ~95%+ effectiveness) 48
. Structural
Coherence, for instance, remains intact (the piece doesn’t collapse under complexity – it still makes sense
and holds together over time), Beauty/Poise remains very high (it might be strange and new, but there’s an
aesthetic brilliance to it, not just a science experiment), and so on. All the standard gates for Tier-20
perfection pass 48
– no reliance on untransformed templates, no dead sections, clarity is maintained
despite risk, etc. In short, it’s both unprecedented and flawless in execution.
- Design test summary: The track functions as an event in music, not just another excellent piece – it
reveals new territory that others can explore. It is apocryphal in the sense that, before it, such a combination
wasn’t even considered canonical or possible in the genre. After it, the horizon has expanded. It’s repeatable
(the method can be taught), but it stands at the precipice where beyond it, we don’t have prior art. The
piece is effectively an “event horizon” of innovation, where known frameworks bend or break.
Example: It’s hard to give concrete examples because by nature these are singular. However, consider
something like Arvo Pärt’s introduction of tintinnabuli (one innovation) combined with a radical new
performance context (another) – hypothetically, if Pärt had debuted tintinnabuli in an interactive
19electronic piece where the performers’ heartbeats influenced the tempo (just as an illustration of dual axes:
one harmonic/melodic system, one procedural/tech system). On a more real note: Max Richter’s “Sleep”
could be argued as approaching this: one innovation was the concept of an 8-hour piece designed to
interface with human sleep cycles (contextual/procedural innovation), another was the meticulous tonal
ambient-classical blend that is both music and neuroscience (a formal/compositional innovation in creating
music meant for subconscious listening). Combined, it was not just an album but a new concept of what an
album or performance can be (overnight concerts where the audience is in beds). It certainly was an event
in the modern classical world. Another example: Björk’s Biophilia project (though outside our given artists)
combined an innovation in musical format (album released as interactive apps – procedural/context
innovation) with new compositional techniques (she wrote songs using unconventional scales and custom-
built instruments like the pendulum-controlled “Gravity Harp”). Each on its own was novel; together it was a
multi-axis innovation (music + tech + education) that carried the project. In any case, a rung-20 piece
defines a new, repeatable possibility space while meeting every classical standard of excellence 49
. It is
essentially legendary – the kind of piece that future musicians reference as a milestone.
Rungs 21–23: Icarus Extension (Multi-Axis Apex)
Rungs 21–23 are Icarus tiers – special extensions of the ladder for pieces that sustain multiple axes at their
peak (20/10) simultaneously 6
. These are exceedingly rare and represent not just individual genius but a
total saturation of the music’s dimensions in harmony. Achieving 21+ means the piece doesn’t only innovate
(Tier-20) but does so while also maintaining perfect scores on multiple axes at once over significant
durations.
21 — Icarus 21 (20×3 axes → IT=21)
Design test: The piece sustains three different axes at Tier-20 levels through its most demanding
sections 50
. In practical terms, choose any three of the five axes (SC, MI, BP, EG, CD) – for example,
Structural Coherence, Motivic Integration, and Evolving Grammar – and design the piece such that each of
these three reaches a “20/10” apex (perfection) during at least one extended segment, without the other two
dropping below an excellent level (≈18/20) simultaneously. It’s a juggling act of triple excellence. Key points:
- Axis triad focus: The composition is engineered around showcasing three axes in full bloom. For instance,
if SC, MI, CD are chosen: the form is extraordinarily coherent even as it evolves (SC at 20), the motivic
development is richly recursive and integrated (MI at 20), and the emotional/carry depth is overwhelming –
listeners feel deeply transported (CD at 20). Or, say, MI, BP, EG: the piece might deliver incredibly beautiful
harmonies and spatial design (BP at 20) while every motif is interrelated (MI at 20) and the musical
grammar keeps evolving rule by rule seamlessly (EG at 20). Any combination is possible, but the piece’s
apex sections must exemplify each chosen axis at perfection.
- Apex windows: There should be at least one section or overlapping span (typically during the piece’s
climactic or core passages) where all three targeted axes are performing at their peak in unison 50
. For
example, a ~30-second climax where the thematic material is being developed in a final recursive layer (MI),
the sound is breathtakingly beautiful and balanced (BP), and the piece is shifting into a new self-created
mode without breaking stride (EG) – all at once. These axis apexes are not sequential one after the other
with rest in between; they can trade off lead but are present together.
- Continuity of excellence: During this apex complex, none of the three axes dips below a Tier-18 level
51
. That is, even when one axis is “leading” the musical narrative at a given moment, the other two remain
nearly as strong – the piece doesn’t slack in any of those dimensions. For instance, if motivic integration is
20taking the spotlight (lots of callbacks and variations flying around), the structural coherence isn’t suddenly
loosening or the beauty isn’t suffering; all remain stellar. There are also no gate failures – meaning the
fundamentals (template avoidance, no dead spans, etc.) are all still fine – even in these most challenging
passages.
- No coasting sections: To achieve this, the piece likely foregoes any easy sections where it rests on laurels.
There are no simplistic breakdowns or template-derived interludes where the composer is essentially
coasting (which could drop one of the axes temporarily). Even quieter or simpler moments are executed

- Duration: The combined duration of the sections where this triple-axis apex is maintained should be
substantial – on the order of minutes, not just a fleeting moment 52

the piece where these conditions hold. It could be one long 3-minute span or multiple shorter spans that
add up, but overall the listener spends a good chunk of time hearing the piece operating on three cylinders

- Design mindset: Essentially, the composer at rung 21 must plan a three-axis apex strategy: e.g., “I will
push form, motif, and beauty all to their zenith together.” This might involve, say, a final section where a
theme recurs in layered counterpoint (motivic integration) with a harmonic resolution that is immensely
satisfying and radiant (beauty) while bringing back material in a way that ties the whole structure together
(structure coherence). The risk is high – any slip and one axis might falter – so the form has to be
constructed to support all three: e.g., using the motivic integration to actually reinforce structure (so SC and
MI help each other), or using the evolving grammar to open new avenues for beauty without undermining
coherence. Synergy between axes is key at this level.
Example (hypothetical): Imagine a piece by Hania Rani at her most inspired – a long-form piano suite where
